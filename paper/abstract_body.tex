Genomic prediction involves three objectives that are often conflated:
predictive correlation, calibration to the phenotypic scale, and recovery of
selection candidates. Neural predictors are usually trained by mean squared
error (MSE), predictive ability is commonly reported as Pearson correlation, and
selection ultimately depends on candidate ranking. We use the identity
$\mathrm{MSE}(z_y,z_{\hat y})=2(1-r)$ to implement a stable differentiable
Pearson objective and ask what is gained, and what is not, by aligning training
with correlation. We compared MSE, Pearson, hybrid, and concordance-correlation
losses for seven architectures on 101 public trait--dataset tasks from 12 panels
spanning ten species. Relative to MSE, Pearson training increased raw
correlation by 0.0048 (95\% panel-cluster bootstrap interval 0.0008--0.0091;
exact 12-panel sign-flip $p=0.052$). Raw Pearson predictions were poorly scaled,
but validation-fitted affine calibration removed this penalty while retaining
the correlation gain. The corresponding change in NDCG@$10$ was only 0.0018
($-0.0001$--0.0035), showing that improved global predictive ability did not
substantially alter upper-tail recovery. Effects varied across architectures:
the Transformer showed the clearest positive contrast ($+0.0176$), whereas the
DeepGS-inspired network was negative ($-0.0067$), although the global
interaction was inconclusive. Thus Pearson loss provides a feasible
metric-consistent objective, but predictive correlation, phenotypic calibration,
and selection performance should be treated as distinct targets rather than
assuming that improvement in one transfers to the others.
